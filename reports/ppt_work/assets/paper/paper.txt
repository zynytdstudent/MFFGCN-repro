Abstract—Radio map estimation (RME) is a crucial method
for analyzing spectrum space utilization and network coverage,
serving as an essential tool for the mobile communication. However,
physicalconstraints,security,privacy,andotherissuesoftenrender
some areas inaccessible, resulting in extremely sparse and unevenly
distributed measurement data. To address these challenges, we
propose a multimodal feature fusion graph convolution network
(MFFGCN). The model incorporates a dual-encoder architecture
with an adaptive multi-feature fusion module to exploit environ-
mental information and learn the shadowing effects of radio-signal
propagation. We then convert the coarse estimation into regional
feature patches and construct a graph over these patches. A graph
neural network aggregates contextual information among them,
thereby alleviating the impact of uneven spatial sampling. Exten-
sive experiments on open datasets demonstrate that our method
achieves state-of-the-art performance, effectively reducing the ef-
fects of uneven sampling.
Index Terms—Radio map estimation, uneven spatial distr-
ibution, multimodal fusion, graph neural network.

Radio Map Estimation (RME), also known as Spectrum Car-
tography (SC), can capture signal activity, network coverage,
and spectrum occupancy information [3]. It provides important
support for illegal radiation source management and wireless
network coverage planning and is of great signiﬁcance to the
spectrum security [4]. In recent years, with the rise of new
mobile communication applications, the application scenarios
of radio maps have been further expanded [5]. For example:
radiation source localization, trajectory tracking [6], inter-cell
interference coordination [7] and UAV-based air-ground com-
munication [8]. In these novel applications, the accuracy of RME
is directly related to the ﬁnal effect of the application. There-
fore, accurate and high-resolution RME becomes increasingly
important [9].
A “radio map” generally characterizes the distribution of the
signal’s power spectral density (PSD) over spatial locations and
frequencies, and sometimes over time [10]. It is often built
on crowd-sourced sensor systems such as Electrosense+ [11]
and its predecessor Electrosense [12]. Radio maps are formed
by reconstructing the cumulative power of RF signals received
by sensors and are presented as 2D or 3D “images” in which
each pixel represents the grid to which it belongs, as shown
in Fig. 1(a). Each pixel describes the measured or estimated
value of the Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI), or
sometimes the Reference Signal Received Power (RSRP) is used
instead, depending on the needs of the scene. Early work mainly
used path loss models or data-driven interpolation algorithms.
With the development of deep learning and its wide application
in wireless communications, in recent work, researchers have
used deep learning methods to implement RME. Meanwhile,
current mainstream models usually use autoencoders (AEs) or
U-Net as the backbone. With the rise of Generative Adversarial
Networks (GANs) [13], recent work has introduced GANs. We
will provide a more comprehensive literature review later in
Section II.
Despite many successes, existing solutions still exhibit cer-
tain limitations. In “urban canyons”, complex non-line-of-sight
propagation effects due to tall buildings and vegetation produce

been investigated by existing deep learning-based methods.
In order to solve the above problems, a multimodal feature
fusion graph convolution network (MFFGCN) is proposed in
this paper. We retain the current mainstream encoder-decoder
as a simple and effective feature extraction architecture. To
achieve multimodal feature fusion of sparse observations with
environmental maps, we design a dual-encoder architecture
that includes an adaptive feature fusion module to replace the
encoder part in U-Net. What’s more, We introduce a GCN to
model observation-constrained areas, dynamically constructing
graphs in a patch-like fashion. Our motivation for proposing this
model is that the information transfer principle of GCN makes it
possible to aggregate information over long distances between
different areas to better reconstruct observation-constrained ar-
eas. In addition, we propose a residual output method that makes
the GCN pay more attention to the differences due to sampling
non-uniformity rather than estimating the radio maps directly.
In summary, the main contributions of the paper are as follows:
1) We propose a multimodal feature fusion network contain-

module for better learning of shading effects and signal
propagation laws from sparse observations and environ-
mental maps.
2) We propose a patch-level graph convolution reﬁne net-
work for remotely aggregating features between patches,
thus reducing the impact of observation-constrained re-
gions. The estimation results are then optimized on the
basis of 1 by a residual output method.
3) Extensive experiments are conducted on an authorita-
tive dataset which show that the reconstruction error of
the proposed model is signiﬁcantly improved compared
to previous state-of-the-art approaches, especially in the
observation-constrained case.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Non-Deep Learning-Based RME
In some simple settings, RME can be integrated with path
loss, such as the classic log-distance path-loss (LDPL) model
estimation [16]. Under the assumption that the propagation law
isﬁxed,Leeetal.[17]usedtheleastsquares(LS)methodtosolve
for the unknown parameters and reconstructed the complete
radio map. For more complex settings, researchers prefer spatial
interpolation methods, the most classical of which is based on
kriging [18]. Sato et al. [19] proposed a modiﬁed kriging-based
RME method to realize probabilistic interference constraints in
the propagation environment through quadratic parameter esti-
mation. Besides kriging, well-known examples of such methods
include inverse distance weighting (IDW) [20], thin plate spline
(TPS) [21] and radial basis function (RBF) [22]. Additionally,
there are research outcomes based on traditional machine learn-
ingtechniques[10],includingdictionarylearning[23],Bayesian
learning [24], compressed sensing [25], tensor completion [26]
and k-nearest neighbor [27]. The aforementioned methods often
rely on ideal assumptions such as smoothness and low-rankness,
which unfortunately cannot be satisﬁed by the complex propa-
gation effects in urban.
B. Deep Learning-Based RME
With the development of deep learning and its wide appli-
cation in wireless communications, deep neural network-based
methods have received increasing attention in recent years.
Shrestha et al. [28] proposed a method based on tensor decom-
position that reconstructs separate radio maps for each source
and uses DNNs to learn the image-to-image transformation task.

Much subsequent work has largely followed this framework.
Yang et al. [29] introduced an automatic radio map updating
method based on enhanced transfer learning. Li et al. [30]
proposed a ﬁne-grained radio map transfer method leveraging
a domain classiﬁer. These efforts provide important support for
deep learning-based RME.
Inspired by the success of Convolutional Neural Network
(CNN) in the ﬁeld of image processing, a series of works
have emerged in recent years using U-Net or AE as backbone
networks. Teganya et al. [31] were the ﬁrst to introduce deep
learning methods and developed a fully convolutional deep
completion autoencoder to learn the propagation phenomena
of spatial structures. Levie et al. [32] proposed a RadioUNet
model based on the connection of two U-Nets, and for the ﬁrst
time proposed the use of Tx, building maps and sparse sampling
splices as model inputs, which had a signiﬁcant impact on sub-
sequent work. Hu et al. [33] proposed an unsupervised learning
variant of GAN that utilizes ResNet and dilated convolution in
thepatchGANframeworktolearnRSSdistributionsin3Dspace.
Chenetal.[34]ﬁrstintroducedgraphneuralnetworksintoRME.
They constructed a pixel-level graph based on line-of-sight or
non-line-of-sight, and then reconstructed the radio maps using
graph attention networks (GAT). However, constructing graphs
based on individual pixels results in overly complex graphs,
making them impractical for high-resolution RME. Wang et
al. [35] introduced a diffusion model and improved the estima-
tion performance under sampling-free conditions. However the
huge computational load of the diffusion model is a signiﬁcant
challenge.
All of the above studies are based on purely data-driven
approaches, so they require high data quality. In contrast, re-
cent work has begun to favor the incorporation of physical
propagation knowledge into the model to improve robustness.
Zheng et al. [36] introduced a Pix2Pix-based model and used
residual estimation methods to introduce SPM wireless propa-
gation models. Zhang et al. [37] proposed an approach based
on conditional generative adversarial networks (cGAN) and a
two-stage training method that utilizes a combination of multiple
loss functions to learn the shading details of LDPL models.
For the ﬁrst time, they considered the inhomogeneous sampling
problem and improved model robustness by GAN and signal
propagation model. Jiang et al. [38] introduced the volume
integral equation into the loss function to predict the total electric
ﬁeld based on the mathematical relationship between the total
electric ﬁeld and the incident ﬁeld.
These methods have achieved signiﬁcant success in RME.
However, there is still a gap in the study of observation-
constrained areas. Existing purely data-driven methods assume
that sensors can be uniformly distributed in space and lack
efﬁcient and specialized structures to ensure that they can be
applied to non-uniformly distributed data. While methods based
on propagation models (e.g., RME-GAN) improve robustness
to some extent, there is still a lack of dedicated modules to
address constrained regions. This greatly limits the use of
RME in real scenarios. To address the limitations identiﬁed
above, we introduce MFFGCN, which fuses environmental
context with sparse observations and propagates information
over patch-level graphs—thereby preserving long-range spatial
dependence. The model also remains lightweight and robust in
severely observation-constrained regions.

B. Graph Convolution Reﬁne Network
After obtaining the coarse estimations, we mitigate the effect
of observation-constrained regions through the message passing
mechanism of graph neural networks. Speciﬁcally, our proposed
Graph Convolution Reﬁne Network is shown in Fig. 5. After
obtaining the concatenated feature map from the map and guide
branches, we divide it into non-overlapping spatial patches.
Each patch is then ﬂattened into a feature vector, denoted as
f(i), where i indicates the index of the patch node. These
feature vectors serve as the initial node representations for
graph construction and are subsequently processed by the GCN
to perform feature reﬁnement through neighborhood message
passing. First, sparse observations are utilized to replace part of
the results in Coarse estimation to ensure the accuracy of the
input data.
Two types of features are then extracted through separate
convolutional branches. The map features are generated from
the sparse observation input. Their primary role is to re-inject
the original, high-conﬁdence measurement information into the
reﬁnement stage. This helps the network preserve local accuracy
by anchoring the reﬁnement to ground-truth values in observed
areas.
The guide features, on the other hand, are extracted from the
coarse estimation result. They encode prior knowledge about
the predicted signal distribution and are used to guide the sub-
sequent graph construction. Speciﬁcally, they provide semantic
cues about channel fading patterns and spatial similarity, which
enable the graph to establish meaningful connections between
spatially related or propagation-similar patches.
After concatenation, the two features are passed to the graph
construction module (Map2Graph) and further reﬁned by the
GCN layers. This architecture effectively separates structural
input injection (via map features) from semantic propagation
modeling (via guide features), enabling the network to leverage
both observation-grounded data and learned signal priors during
reﬁnement.
The channel dimensions for both feature types are selected
based on their distinct roles in the reﬁnement framework. The
map features use a single channel, as the sparse observations
contain localized, high-conﬁdence information. Their function
is to retain spatial ﬁdelity rather than encode complex semantics,
making a single-channel design both sufﬁcient and efﬁcient. In
contrast, the guide features require greater expressive capac-
ity to represent coarse-level signal priors and support graph
construction. Based on empirical tuning, we adopt 81 channels
for the guide features, which provides a good balance between
representational power and computational cost, with negligible
performance improvement observed beyond this setting.

A simple but important design of this paper is to estimate
radio maps in a residual manner. We treat the output of the graph
convolution reﬁne network as a residual map rather than a direct
estimation. This is motivated by two practical considerations.
First, the coarse estimation already captures the global propa-
gation structure and major signal patterns, leaving the residuals
to model local shading details and corrections—particularly in
observation-constrained regions. Second, due to the patch-based
graph construction, the pixel-wise alignment is partially lost
during the reﬁnement process, making direct regression of the
full-resolution map from the GCN less effective. By modeling

This design allows the reﬁnement network to better complement
the coarse estimation without attempting to fully reconstruct the
radio map.

V. EXPERIMENTS
In this section, we evaluate and compare MFFGCN’s results
on an authoritative data set, particularly in the presence of
observation-constrained areas.
A. Dataset Description
The experiments are based on the RadioMapSeer data set[32],
which contains simulated radio maps, building maps, and trans-
mitter maps with both 2D and 3D scenes. In the experiments, we
consider only 2D scenes and use the building maps in the data
set to replace the environment maps in the framework shown
in Fig. 2. The city maps in this data set were obtained from
OpenStreetMap and radio maps were obtained through WIn-
Prop. The radio maps include three radio propagation simulation
models: DPM, IRT2 and IRT4. The city maps in the data set
include Ankara, Berlin, Glasgow, Ljubljana, London and Tel
Aviv. Speciﬁcally, the data set consists of 701 city maps of size
256 × 256 m2, with a resolution of 1 m. 80 potential transmitters
are considered in each map. Each transmitter is 1.5 meters high
with an isotropic antenna. It transmits wireless signals with
23 dBm of power at 5.9 GHz. More speciﬁc parameters of the
data set are shown in Table II.
Among the available simulation modalities in the Ra-
dioMapSeer dataset (DPM, IRT2, IRT4), we adopt the DPM
(Dominant Path Model) to ensure compatibility with prior
works. In particular, DPM is used as the standard input modality
in RadioUNet [32], and is also adopted in several recent top-tier
studies on deep learning-based radio map estimation [35], [37],
[38]. These works demonstrate that DPM strikes a favorable bal-
ance between physical realism and signal smoothness, offering
stable learning behavior and reproducible results.
To maintain consistency with baseline settings, we follow
the same data preprocessing steps as implemented in the
RadioUNet. Speciﬁcally, all radio maps are ﬁrst normalized
to the [0, 1] range based on the original path loss values
([−127, −47] dB). A thresholding operation is then applied
to remove pixels with normalized values below 0.2, which
correspond to extremely weak or noisy signals. The remaining
values are re-normalized to [0, 1] to restore dynamic range.
Each sample consists of three spatially aligned inputs: a sparse
observation map, a binary environment mask, and a transmitter
location map. The sparse observation is generated by sampling
a subset of signal values from the DPM radio map according
to predeﬁned densities or observation constraints (see Sec-
tion V-B), following the setting used in RadioUNet. No further
augmentation or transformation is applied. This preprocessing
strategy ensures fair comparison with prior methods and sup-
ports consistent evaluation under different sampling scenarios.
B. Experiment Settings
Referring to the setup in [32], we divide the training set,
validation set and test set in the ratio of 5:1:1. Speciﬁcally, data
from 500 maps are used for training, 100 for validation, and
the last 100 for testing. This ensures that the maps in the test
set are never seen by the model and can effectively verify the
generalization of the model across different cities.
Considering that the number of actual observations is ex-
tremely sparse and unstable, we set the minimum number of
observable samples per map to 10 and the maximum to 300 (less
than 0.5% of the total). The experiments are repeated ﬁve times
with different random seeds, and we report the mean ± standard
deviation across these runs. For the observation-constrained
region, from the practical application point of view, we consider
that it is usually located at the edge position of the map, so its
range is abstracted as a rectangular region close to the edge in the
experimentaldesign. Thatis,werandomlyappendregionstoone
side of the map (top, bottom, left, or right), and all sampled data
within this region will be eliminated. So the model must rely on
sampled data from other regions to recover the complete radio
map. In fact, the different region shapes have almost no effect
on the experimental results, as we will discuss in Section V-F.
In order to verify the performance of the model in uniform and

Fig. 16 shows the results of the impact of missing building

The sensitivity analysis reveals two representative failure
scenarios. (1) Severe map noise: injecting 1% impulse noise into
the building map increases the NMSE of MFFGCN by 145%,
whereas RadioUNet and RME-GAN surge by 160% and 182%,
respectively, conﬁrming that MFFGCN is still the most robust
under heavy corruption. (2) Abrupt structural changes: down-

or randomly omitting four buildings raises the NMSE of every
method by almost a factor of four. Although the dynamic-graph
module mitigates part of the damage, MFFGCN degrades at a
rate comparable to the baselines, exposing its limited adaptabil-
ity to highly dynamic layouts.
In summary, both map noise and rapid environmental vari-
ation substantially reduce performance. MFFGCN remains the
most resilient under noise, but improving its responsiveness to
dynamic scenes will require on-the-ﬂy map updates and online
ﬁne-tuning, which we leave for future work.

VI. CONCLUSION
Recovering radio maps from sparse and unbalanced sampling
is the key to spectrum management and optimization, which
is important for reducing frequency conﬂicts and promoting
the development of mobile communications. In this study, we
proposed an MFFGCN. To effectively combine sparse sampling
and information in the environment, we designed a multi-feature
fusion network based on dual encoders and adaptive feature
fusion modules. To balance the uneven sampling, we designed
a reﬁne network based on graph convolution with a residual
output. This design enables the graph convolution to aggre-
gate the information between patches and reduce the effect of
observation-constrained regions. Extensive experiments demon-
strated that our method clearly outperformed state-of-the-art
methods, especially in the setting of observation-constrained
regions. Our model reduced the RMSE by 6.67% and the
NMSE by 11.76% compared to the state-of-the-art model at
25% of the restricted regions, while requiring 14.5% fewer
FLOPs and virtually the same per-sample latency (5.17 ms)
as the lightweight RadioUNet baseline. At the same time, we
also acknowledge the limitation of the model, which is overly
dependent on environmental information. In the future, we will
further improve the model to adapt to different scenarios.
